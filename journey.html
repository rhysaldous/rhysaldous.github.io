The resilient journey of Rhys Aldous from hardship to financial insight

From adversity to a business-development desk. The jobs were catering and construction. The lesson was money.

Rhys Aldous · writer, West Sussex

Also on Medium: original post

In this piece I take you through my own path: nights on the street, then work, then a desk in business development. It was not a straight line. The jobs were in different sectors. Each one taught something about finance, resilience, and what “success” actually costs.

Early work

I did not arrive in finance by a graduate scheme. I started in the jobs that keep a city running — catering and construction. Kitchen porter. Bricklayer. Each role taught hard work, tenacity, and the habit of watching what things actually cost.

Catering and hospitality

I started as a kitchen porter and worked up to assistant chef. That is where cost management stops being a slogan. Waste is money. Staffing is money. A customer who does not come back is money. Those are the same problems a finance desk pretends it invented.

Construction and trades

Carpenter, electrician, labourer. Project management is not a PowerPoint. It is whether the materials arrive, whether the quote was honest, and whether you can still eat if the job overruns. Budgeting and risk assessment were not theory. They were the week.

Nights without a home

The hardest stretch was having nowhere to sleep. Those nights were ugly and they were useful. They put a price on stability that no textbook manages. I do not romanticise it. I will not pretend it did not happen.

Business development

Moving from that to business development was not overnight. It was persistence and a refusal to treat the earlier jobs as a secret. Different trades taught different markets. Hospitality taught relationships. Construction taught a quote that survives contact with reality.

Financial insight does not only come from a dealing floor. It comes from a kitchen that cannot afford to waste stock and a site that cannot afford a wrong order. That is the point of this essay, and it is why I still write.
